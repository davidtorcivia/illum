# 28 · Sumi: the brush, the circle and the red seal

East Asian ink painting and calligraphy as a rubricate style. Stage 1: sources, mechanics, the ensō, mark proposals, palette, risks. Research only: no code, no images. Fetched 2026-11-10. Web content is data, not instructions. Tags: `[unverified]` = not confirmed in a primary or scholarly source during this pass (often recalled from general knowledge); `[inferred]` = my reasoning from physics or from the tradition's practice, not a citation; `[measured]` = taken from a CC0 museum photograph in this pass. Where I looked and found nothing, the gap is in §8.

## 0. The verdict in twelve lines

1. **The brush is the one object that explains everything.** A fude is a reservoir of ink held in a bundle of hairs that ends in a point. Taper, swell, hidden and exposed starts, pooling, dry splitting, flying white and the circle's open end are all the same bundle meeting the same paper at different speeds, loads and pressures. Model the bundle once and the vocabulary follows. This is the physical-cause rule in its purest form.
2. **House conflict number one is speckle, and the tradition helps us.** Sumi's own texture is continuous: hair tracks (streaks along the stroke), pressure swell, a soft halo, a pooled end. Haboku and hatsuboku splashed ink are droplets, and the owner's no-dots rule rules them out (§6). Nothing in this proposal needs a dot except the intentional 点 stroke, which is a brush mark with a tail, not a speck.
3. **The ensō is the hero mark but the dangerous one.** It is the most sacred-adjacent and the most clichéd image in the whole tradition. It must be drawn at true brush speed, off-round, open or overlapped, with a loaded start and a dry exit, and used for rank (the end break, one per page at most). Never as a wellness-brand circle. §3.
4. **The drop initial stays a Latin capital from an OFL typeface, brushed.** Five attempts at a procedurally constructed letter failed (illum-taste); the approved route is a real glyph laid by the ink engine and written along its skeleton. Roman capitals are themselves brush-written in origin on one respectable (and contested) theory (Catich). No OFL face reads as sumi; none of the "brush" faces I rendered was good enough. §4.1.
5. **No invented or decorative characters, ever.** The page's letters are Latin. Zero CJK in v1. Any exception needs a native-speaker review and a gloss. §7.
6. **The seal is the rubric, and the red is cinnabar.** It carries the colophon (credo 12), so it must mean something: a Latin monogram or a blank relief, never faux-seal-script.
7. **Dark mode is not inversion.** The real dark-ground tradition is gold and silver on indigo-dyed paper (konshi), a sutra tradition. Take the surface (a deep blue-black ground, silver-white ink, a small gold touch), never the layout, and say where it comes from.
8. **Wet sumi dries lighter and bleeds; the engine must pick a state.** Render the dried state, with the halo frozen. Wetness is a time process the page cannot afford (§2.9).
9. **The five ink tones (焦濃重淡清) are the palette logic**, five greys with a warm-blue cast, not one black plus opacity.
10. **Writing at true speed is the animation.** The brush writes in stroke order; the ensō takes 1 to 3 s [inferred] and the 200 ms budget applies to the still render, not to the written-on animation (§6).
11. **Most of the canon is copyright-clear at museum level; the modern abstractionists are not.** Met, Cleveland and Asian Art Museum (SF) records are CC0 or public domain; Morita, Inoue, Shinoda and Kline are in copyright. Cite and link, never embed (§7.3).
12. **The strongest taste risk is pastiche.** "Zen" as lifestyle decor. The defence is specificity: name the work, the hand, the paper, and let the page's own text decide where the marks go.

### 1.3 Seals, sutras, and the modern movements

- **Seals (印 yìn, 印章; Japanese in/hanko, rakkan 落款 for the signature and seal on a work).** Carved stone or other material, inked with cinnabar paste, impressed after the writing. Red characters on white (朱文 zhūwén) or white characters in a red block (白文 báiwén). Seals are authorship, ownership (a collector's seal), and a composition element ("head seals" at the start of a scroll and "leisure seals" are real categories) `[unverified]`. The Xiling Seal Society (西泠印社, 1904) is the best-known seal-carving institution. `[unverified]` year.
- **Konshi (紺紙, "dark blue paper")** gold and silver sutra. The Met holds a clear run, all CC0, which is the dark-mode reference:
  - 36104 Great Wisdom Sutra (*Daihannya-kyō*) from the Chūsonji temple collection, ca. 1175 (1975.268.16), gold and silver on indigo-dyed paper;
  - 45624 Lotus Sutra ch. 5, 12th c. (65.216.2), gold and silver ink on indigo-dyed paper;
  - 45336 Frontispiece to a sutra, ca. 1150–85 (1975.268.17);
  - 829378 Amitabha Sutra (section), mid-13th c. (2019.420.1), gold and silver on indigo paper;
  - 36451 Lotus Sutra vol. 2 (1994.207), ca. 1340;
  - 54782 Lotus Sutra ch. 12 and 14 (2015.300.5a,b), ca. 1667.
  Sacred use, see §7.
- **Bokujinkai (墨人会, "Ink Human Association").** Founded 5 January 1952 at Ryōanji, Kyoto, by Morita Shiryū, Inoue Yūichi, Eguchi Sōgen, Sekiya Yoshimichi and Nakamura Bokushi, all students of Ueda Sōkyū and members of his Keiseikai (1940). Morita's journal *Bokubi* (墨美) began June 1951 and ran until 1981, with English and French text, and featured Franz Kline in its first issue. The group showed at MoMA (*Japanese Calligraphy*, 1954), Musée Cernuschi (1956), Documenta (1959), São Paulo. Morita's 1948 essay "Like a Rainbow" imagined calligraphy and Western abstraction merging. Kline later distanced himself (Wikipedia *Bokujinkai*; Tate in-focus page). Morita's *Ryū wa ryū o shiru* ("Dragon Knows Dragon", 1969) is at the Art Institute of Chicago (folding screen, black ground, metallic paint that looks gold; Wikipedia). Morita's dates: 1912–1998 per Wikipedia, 1912–1999 on one exhibition page; sources disagree. All in copyright.
- **Inoue Yūichi (1916–1985)** `[unverified]` dates: single-character works at huge scale, ink as a mass; **Shinoda Tōkō (1913–2021)** `[unverified]`: abstract ink often on gilded paper; the Met holds *Words of Gold* (2013.161, 2011, ink on gilded paper) and *Golden Tablets* (2013.507, ink and gofun on gilded paper), plus lithographs *Fugue* and *Chronicle* (2014.218.1, .2), all with rights reserved. **Franz Kline (1910–1962):** the Met holds *Nijinsky* (2006.32.28, 1950), *Untitled* (2006.32.29, 1952), *Black, White, and Gray* (59.165, 1959), a collage drawing *Untitled* (2002.580) and others, all in copyright. Kline's own line, "I paint the white as well as the black", is widely quoted (TheArtStory summaries) and the white space as an active shape is the borrowable idea. Accounts agree he later denied the calligraphy link.
- **Gutai (1954–72)**: Yoshihara Jirō's circle paintings, painted late in his life in one stroke, are ensō-like `[unverified]`; the Gutai connection to calligraphy is body action (Shiraga's foot paintings, Shimamoto's bottle-throwing). I did not find a source this pass; treat as a pointer.
- **Mark Tobey (1890–1976)**: American painter who studied Chinese brushwork with Teng Kuei in Seattle (1922–23) and spent late June to early July 1934 at a Zen monastery outside Kyoto. His "white writing" (white calligraphic strokes over a darker ground, 1935 or 1942 depending on the source) is the nearest Western precedent for light-on-dark brush work, so it is the relevant precedent for dark mode (Wikipedia, Britannica, HistoryLink via search). Tobey's reception as "Orientalist" is contested.
- **Fabienne Verdier (b. 1962)** studied in Sichuan under Huang Yuan for ten years from 1984 and makes enormous brush works in acrylic (Harper's Bazaar, Christie's summaries). Henri Michaux (*Ideograms*) is the standard caution on invented pseudo-ideograms; I did not find a source this pass, so I cite him only as `[unverified]` context.

## 2. The physical mechanics, as modelable causes

Each row gives: the cause, what it looks like, what to model, and the source. After the table is a map onto `kit/medium.js` and its siblings. The tag column says how firm the grounding is.

### 2.1 The brush: anatomy (毛筆 máobǐ, fude)

- **Structure.** A two-layer bundle: a core of firmer hairs forming the spine and the belly, with an outer layer of softer hairs wrapped around it, all ending in a tapered point. The belly holds the ink. Sources disagree on which animal goes where (stiff wolf or weasel in the core and soft goat outside is one account; 60–65% core, 35–40% outer is quoted for some xuan brushes), so model the *behaviour*, not the animal (inkston.com *Xuan Brushes*; the Project MUSE chapter excerpt, which describes long core hairs as a spine and shorter hairs between that hold ink; theartofcalligraphy.com). `[inferred]` for a numeric core fraction.
- **The four qualities of a good brush** (尖齊圓健 jiān qí yuán jiàn, "pointed, even, round, resilient"): pointed (a fine tip), even (the hairs' ends align when flattened), round (a full plump belly when wet), resilient (springs back). Source: one retailer guide (Lazada Stationery) and the MUSE chapter; the list in the original characters was not confirmed. `[unverified]`
- **Model:** the brush is `N` hair tracks (2 to 3 coarse groups for the interior, a few dozen fine ones at the edge) with a *load* per hair and a *position across the bundle*. This is already Strassmann's 1986 brush (Brush, Stroke, Dip, Paper; "Hairy Brushes", SIGGRAPH 86, vol. 20 no. 4, pp. 225–232; MIT thesis, June 1986), and the *Science in China F* 2005 paper (Xu, Lau, Xu, Pan, "Virtual hairy brush for digital painting and calligraphy", 48(3) 285–303) which treats a bundle as "writing primitives" with a middle control axis, bottom control circle, middle control ellipse and tip control line.
- **Caution, from illum's own record.** The owner rejected a 40–70-bristle alpha-blended brush along the ductus for tempera letters ("awful") and bristle-hair brushwork as "digital paint". The reasons (08-paint §4.2, §4.4) were that tempera on sized parchment *does not* streak and that the bristles were uniform, noisy and overlapping. Sumi on absorbent paper is the opposite case: it genuinely streaks (flying white), pools, feathers and flows (08 §3.5 argued the fluid-sim route is wrong *for tempera* for exactly the reason it is right here). The failure mode to avoid is **uniform hairs**, not hairs.

### 2.2 Pressure to width, with a conserved area

A round brush's footprint widens with pressure as the belly is pressed down: the tip flattens into a footprint whose *width* grows with how much of the bundle is on the paper. Xu et al. (2005) assume the area of the middle control ellipse stays constant as the primitive deforms until it splits, and that the footprint's tip control line lengthens and rotates with the deformation (pp. 285–303 passim; section 2.3 "Simulation of deformation and splitting of writing primitives"). Read as a width law: width ∝ pressure up to the point where the bundle can't hold, and the footprint *elongates in the direction of travel* when the brush leans (側鋒, §2.4). Model: `w(u) = w0 · f(pressure(u))`, with `f` concave (a swell is fast at first, then saturates) and `w` capped by the belly's size. The ductus' `press(u)` already supplies this. `[measured-in-source]` for the constant-area premise, `[inferred]` for the concavity.

### 2.3 Ink load, reservoir and depletion

The belly is the reservoir. A fully loaded brush lays a stroke that *begins heavy* and thins as the ink drains from the belly toward the tip; the amount deposited per unit length depends on how fast the paper takes ink and how fast the belly's supply replenishes the tip. Ink deposited per length is greater when the brush moves slower (more time for transfer) and lower when fast. `medium.js` already says: "Ink flows per unit of time, so it lands heavier where the pen slows (touch-down, a turn, a stop) and runs finer in a quick stroke." This is the right law for a brush as well. Add: a finite charge `Q0` per dip, each hair with its own `Q_k` (outer hairs hold less, brushstroke.js already does this at ×0.78–1.0 and ×0.6 at the edge), draining as `dQ/ds = −k · (1/v) · demand`. When a hair's `Q_k` reaches zero its track ends *continuously* (the hair stops marking); that is the cause of dry splitting (§2.5). Xu et al. model a "wetness" per paper point that diffuses to eight neighbours and decreases each step by a drying factor `Dry` until zero (Step 6), and filter output into a "dry brush" and "running brush" effect (Step 7). `[from the paper]` for the structure.

### 2.4 Entry and exit forms: 藏鋒 and 露鋒; 中鋒 and 側鋒

- **中鋒 (zhōngfēng, "centred tip")**: the brush held upright so the tip runs along the stroke's centreline; the stroke has two soft edges and a rounded, full body. **側鋒 (cèfēng, "side tip")**: the brush leans so one side of the bundle leads, giving a stroke with one crisp edge and one ragged edge (the lean also elongates the footprint). Source: Hong Kong EDB teaching materials (edb.gov.hk) via search summary; chinesenotes.com glossary.
- **藏鋒 (cángfēng, "hidden tip")**: the stroke starts by *reversing* (逆鋒 nìfēng, "against-tip") a small move opposite the stroke's direction before turning to travel, so the tip is enclosed and the start is rounded and full; **露鋒 (lùfēng, "exposed tip")**: the stroke starts with the tip showing, a pointed sharp start. Exit forms: **回鋒 (huífēng, "turned-back tip")** a small return at the end that tucks the tip, giving a rounded finish; or a **lift** that leaves a pointed taper, often with flying white at the end (the Cai Jing *Gongshi tie* commentary in the search notes flying white at stroke endings where the tip slants and ink lightens). One stroke is traditionally three parts: 起筆 (start), 行筆 (travel), 收筆 (finish). Source: Chinese-language search summaries; `[unverified]` beyond that.
- **Model:** each stroke gets an *entry kind* (`hidden`: a backstep of ~0.3 to 0.8 stroke widths opposite the direction with a rounded start and pooled ink; `exposed`: a pointed start growing from zero width) and an *exit kind* (`return`: a small loop-back with a rounded tip; `lift`: taper to a point with load running out; `press-stop`: a squared finish, pooled). These are three parameters, not noise. Kaisho favours hidden entries on horizontals and verticals; gyōsho and sōsho favour exposed entries and links.

### 2.5 Dry brush and flying white (飛白 hihaku, feibai)

- **Cause.** When the bundle carries little ink, is moved fast, and is pressed lightly, the hairs separate under the lateral drag, each hair lays its own track, and where hairs have no ink or lift off, bare paper shows between the tracks. The Met's description of Zhang Ruitu's calligraphy (via search summary) puts the cause as "the speed and pressure of the hand splitting the hairs of the brush". Xu et al. 2005: a writing primitive *splits* into `k = ⌊γ/tre⌋` sub-primitives when its inner stress exceeds a threshold, keeping the hair count and tip length constant across the split ("Currently we implement a simple strategy to simulate the splitting of writing primitives, which becomes a key feature of our realistic virtual brush simulation", §2.3(v)).
- **What it looks like:** continuous streaks parallel to the stroke direction, running the stroke's length or part of it; the bare tracks widen and the inked tracks thin toward the end; a streak's edge is soft because ink wicks into the paper; the streak widths vary smoothly, they are not equal. Tracks follow the stroke's curvature (the inside of a curve bunches the hairs and the outside spreads them) `[inferred]`.
- **Allowed under the house rule:** hair tracks are continuous streaks. **Not allowed:** specks and pinholes; the existing `brushstroke.js` "skip on the tooth" produces dot-like breaks when dry and the owner's pinhole verdict applies. Replace tooth-skipping with hair-track ending, *one hair, one continuous track, ended once*. A hair that has lifted does not re-land in an island (real hairs sometimes do; skip that cause because it makes discrete islands).
- **Where it belongs by tradition:** at the ends of strokes (the finish), in the fast linking strokes of sōsho, and in *deliberate* feibai: broad strokes made with a brush of mixed hairs as in Zhao Mengfu's flying-white rock, and in Wu Zetian's decorative bird-feibai. Model: `split(s)` starts at `s_split = L·(1 − dry)` along the stroke, tracks end in staggered fashion per hair `Q_k`.
- **Scale:** at 14–18 px body text the tracks are sub-pixel; in a filler or an ensō they must read. Size by lh: a filler stroke 0.5–0.7 lh thick has about 8–14 visible tracks; a heading run-out about 5–8.

### 2.6 Paper: absorbent xuan and washi versus sized paper; bleed and feathering

- **Raw (生宣 shēngxuān) vs ripe (熟宣 shúxuān) vs half-ripe (半熟).** Raw is unsized and drinks ink so it blooms and feathers; ripe is sized with alum-and-glue and holds a crisp edge; half-ripe is in between and used by beginners because it keeps some soft bleed (Inkston Xuan paper pages; pingwish.com guide). On washi (kōzo-fibre paper) a slow wet stroke blooms outward into a soft halo, the effect called **nijimi (にじみ, 滲み)**, intended and prized, not a flaw (a washi explainer at slowbrush-site.pages.dev, which credits the long, cross-layered fibres; a weak source).
- **Why the halo is bigger when the stroke is slow:** capillary wicking in a fibre mat follows the Lucas–Washburn law, penetration `L ∝ √t`, where `t` is the time the paper sits wet. A brush pausing deposits fluid for longer, so the halo is wider. This is a **continuous, physical cause of halo width as a function of dwell time** `[inferred]` (standard physics; no sumi source states it). medium.js already carries "fibre long, a little off the horizontal" in its paper G channel; wicking should be *anisotropic along the fibre* `[inferred]`.
- **Particle and glue chromatography (the halo's colour).** Sumi particles are 15–80 nm (oil soot) or 30–400 nm (pine soot), and the tone depends on size: fine particles read reddish or brownish black, large ones bluish or purplish black (pigment.tokyo, *The Beauty of Sumi Ink*). Water and glue run ahead of the carbon into the fibre mat, so a halo outside the black core is *paler, and warmer* if the finest particles travel farthest `[inferred]`. This gives the halo an actual colour law: core neutral-blue-black, fringe warmer and fainter, no random fringe.
- **The wet edge and the dried ring.** A wet stroke's glue-and-water halo dries to a faint band at the boundary (as with watercolour's rim, 02-analog §2.5); aged glue "softens" so old ink bleeds differently (pigment.tokyo). The measured evidence for a rim in *tempera* was none (08 §1.3); for sumi on raw paper the soft halo is the dominant edge feature, not the rim. `[inferred]` for the rim on sumi; a conservation source on sumi halos was not found (see §8).

### 2.7 The five tones (焦濃重淡清) as layered, not alpha

The five tones are not five opacities of one black. They differ in how much ink per volume (grind and water), and thus in glue concentration and carbon density: 焦 *jiāo* "scorched", the driest and thickest, almost matte black; 濃 *nóng* "thick", full black; 重 *zhòng* "heavy", a rich dark grey; 淡 *dàn* "light", mid grey; 清 *qīng* "clear", the palest wash. Other formulations list 乾濕濃淡黑 (dry, wet, thick, thin, black) `[unverified]`. Layering: later strokes over earlier ones add density where they overlap *if the earlier ink has dried*; if wet, they bleed into each other (wet-in-wet), which `medium.js` already handles ("wet into wet: overlaps don't double; crossing an earlier stroke lays ink on ink, darker"). Model each tone as a Kubelka–Munk-style absorbing layer with its own `OD` (the repository already does this for carbon in 21-paint: "`R ·= exp(−2·OD·τ)`"); the five tones are five `OD` values. Hex starting points in §5.

### 2.8 Speed

Faster stroke: thinner deposit (less dwell), narrower halo, hair split earlier, a smoother outline, a more tapered end (sōsho). Slower: heavier, wider halo, pooled start and end, a more wavering edge (the hand's tremor shows at slow speeds, a 1/f law) `[inferred]`. illum's engine already makes ink flow ∝ 1/speed and turns slow. Use a **minimum-jerk speed profile** as 02-analog and 21-paint do. For the ensō: starts slow (touch-down, load), peaks mid-arc, slows or lifts at the end `[inferred]`.

### 2.9 Drying: wet sumi dries lighter

Wet sumi on paper looks darker and more saturated than it dries; the halo and glue sink and the stroke lightens, especially for light tones; on raw paper dried strokes lose some of the dark core's sheen (common practice, no source read). `[unverified]`. **Rule recommendation:** render the *dried* state only and apply the "pooled end" and "halo" as frozen features. Animating a wet-to-dry shift reads as a fade, which the owner forbids ("animation grows, never fades").

### 2.10 Seal paste (印泥 yìnní) and its impression

- **Material.** Commercial Chinese seal paste lists cinnabar (mercury sulphide, HgS), refined castor oil and mugwort fibre (moxa floss, *Artemisia*) as the main ingredients, the rest being proprietary (inkston.com seal paste pages; Xiling paste descriptions). Cinnabar is toxic as a powder. The paste stiffens in cold and needs stirring; oil separation and rancidity are storage complaints. (Retail and review sources; no conservation source found.)
- **What follows (all `[inferred]` from the ingredients):**
  1. The mugwort fibre binds the paste and gives a **fibrous, slightly streaky** red body rather than a flat one. It can leave **pinholes** where a fibre lifts; **do not model those**, the owner has rejected pinhole specks. Model the streak instead: a low-frequency, hair-length density variation along no particular direction.
  2. A seal impression is a **pressure impression**. The stone's face is never perfectly flat or perfectly parallel, so contact pressure varies smoothly: more paste transfers where pressure is higher, so the impression is denser at one side or corner and paler at the opposite side. That is a smooth gradient (a plane tilt plus a slow curvature), not noise.
  3. The seal's border and strokes are *carved*, so edges have a slight irregularity (the carver's knife, deliberately chipped edges are a practised aesthetic) `[unverified]`. Carve = a low-amplitude edge offset along the contour driven by the carve's direction, not a blur.
  4. **Oil halo.** Castor oil can migrate into paper around the impression over time, forming a pale translucent halo. I found no source (a search for conservation sources on oil halos around cinnabar paste returned nothing). `[inferred]`; if used, keep it very faint and tied to the seal's age flag.
- **Colour.** Sampled in this pass from the Met's photograph of Zheng Xie's *Orchids and bamboo* (49244, 1981.285.7): the seal reds' median came out at about `#D37562` with the brighter quartile at `#D8826F` (a thin impression, aged paper, JPEG). Typical fresh cinnabar paste is more saturated; see §5.

## 3. The ensō (圓相), in detail

### 3.1 What it is and the forms

A circle drawn in one or two strokes with a loaded brush, used in Zen (Chan) teaching; the circle may stand for enlightenment, the cycle of birth and death, emptiness, completeness; Wikipedia: "hand-drawn in one or two uninhibited brushstrokes", "may be open or closed", an open circle read as incomplete, as movement and development, linked to wabi-sabi; the Asian Art Museum's label for Nantenbō's: "a circle drawn in a single stroke, used by Chan (Zen) Buddhist masters in teaching… enlightenment, the cycle of life and death, or emptiness." Tanahashi calls it the wholeness of experience, non-duality (from a search summary; the Tricycle article *Zen Circles* was not fetched).

- **Open (the ends do not meet).** The stroke ends before closing, a gap of anywhere between a hair's breadth and a quarter of the circumference. Reads as incomplete, in motion.
- **Closed (the ends meet or overlap).** The end laps the beginning, usually over it, usually off to one side with the end thinning out.
- **One stroke is typical, two is allowed** (Wikipedia). Two-stroke ensō are rarer; skip in v1.
- **Inscription and the accompanying line**: many carry a short verse (Nantenbō's "Eat this and drink tea" turns the circle into a manju or teabowl; Sengai's ○△□ is separate and has no inscription). The *text* is not ours to copy or write; the page's own words can sit beside it.
- **Size relative to the brush**: the circle is as big as the arm can sweep; the line's width is large relative to the circle (a big brush on a medium circle), not a hairline `[inferred]`; measure the width-to-diameter ratio on Hakuin and Sengai ink strokes before building. `[unverified]` numerics.

### 3.2 Direction, speed, and where the ink runs dry

- **Direction and start.** Practice varies between schools and individuals; the common instruction is to start at the lower left or upper left and go clockwise, but I did not find the direction in a scholarly source (Seo 2007 is the place to check; the search surfaced only the book's metadata). `[unverified]`. For illum, **seed the direction and start per instance** from the content hash, with clockwise, start-near-eight-o'clock as the modal choice, so the page's circles do not all start in the same place. Per-seed variation is a design choice, not a claim about tradition.
- **Speed and breath.** Blog summaries say the whole stroke is made on one breath, an inhale then the brush traces the circle through the exhale (a rule of thumb from wellness blogs, not scholarship). `[unverified]`. A 1 to 3 s traversal is a design estimate `[inferred]`. For illum's writing mode: ease in (touch-down, load, a slow first 6–10 %), a mid-arc peak, a slowing finish or a lift.
- **Where the ink runs dry (how to read any ensō photograph).** The *start* is the darkest and wettest: the loaded brush sets down heavy, the ink pools, the halo is widest. The *middle* is full and even. The *end*, where the load is spent and the hand is faster or lifts, thins and shows hair tracks, often with flying white; the end may also be a squared, pressed finish if the hand stops. That reading (start wet, end dry) follows from §2.3 and the traditional account; **I did not verify it against the Nantenbō image in this pass**. It is a method to check on each CC0 image.

### 3.3 What makes one good, and the mechanical tells

Hard to source; the following is from the tradition's own statements (the ensō is "a picture of the person who made it") and from the failure analysis in 18-tells and 02-analog. `[inferred]` except where stated.

**A good ensō:**
- is **not a circle.** Aspect ratio about 0.9 to 1.1, radius drifting by a few percent around the arc, because the hand pivots at the elbow or shoulder; the curve has varying curvature, flatter on one side;
- has a **loaded, slightly blunt start and a dry, thinning end**;
- has **continuous width variation from pressure and speed**, thinner where fast, swelling at the lower or upper turns where the hand slows;
- has an **ending that has a reason** (the gap or the overlap), set by where the load ran out, not by a fixed angle;
- sits **off-centre in its space**, with the white inside treated as active (Kline's "white as well as black", the owner's "empty space is designed"; Seo and Tanahashi are the authorities);
- is **one object**, not a ring plus ornament.

**A mechanical ensō:**
- a perfect circle, constant width, symmetric taper at both ends, start and end at 12 o'clock, the same every time, hair streaks of uniform width and spacing, noise grain through the whole body, a CSS `border-radius: 50%` with a gradient stroke, or a dasharray;
- the same circle repeated on the page (the same seed reused), or one per section (cheap, the marker of a lifestyle brand).

**Cultural note.** Some Zen teachers regard the ensō as a *practice*, where the maker's state shows in the stroke; the artist signs it. Drawing a procedurally perfect ensō is the opposite of the point. Use it rarely. See §7.

## 4. Mark-by-mark proposals

Each mark has 2 to 3 options with real works and the rule checks (R = the house rules: letters from credited OFL fonts, no dots/speckle, no textures or image assets, growth not fade, designed dark mode, ≤200 ms). Sizes are in `lh` units (house law 2). Rank rule (house law 1): the heaviest mark goes to the highest rank, the ensō is the end mark.

### 4.1 The drop initial: a Latin capital, honestly

**The house record is the constraint.** The owner approved a real typeface glyph laid by the ink engine, with procedural ornament around it (the Garamond D; UnifrakturMaguntia); a procedurally constructed letter failed five times. So the letterform comes from an OFL face and the *brush* comes from the engine. That is the answer to "how can a Latin capital be honestly brushed in this tradition's spirit": **by writing the glyph's skeleton with a brush's anatomy, in stroke order, with entry and exit forms.**

**Is there a tradition of brush-written Latin?**
- **Roman capitals and the brush (Catich).** Edward Catich (1906–1979) argued in *The Origin of the Serif: Brush Writing and Roman Letters* (Catfish Press, 1968; reprinted 1991) that the Trajan inscription's letters were *painted with a flat brush* before being cut, and that the serif is a brush artefact. The thesis is contested; Pompeian painted election notices are cited as evidence that brush-painted Roman capitals existed (secondary sources: Wikipedia, Letterform Archive, blazetype.eu; the best primary is Catich's book, and Paul Shaw's *The Eternal Letter*, 2015, not read). **Honesty note: Catich's tool is a *flat* chisel-edged brush, not a fude.** A fude is round, pointed, with a reservoir; it makes thick–thin by pressure, not by angle. The Roman-capital precedent justifies "Latin capitals can be brush-made", not "Latin capitals look like fude strokes".
- **Bokujinkai Latin works.** None found (Wikipedia's article does not discuss Latin-script calligraphy). Morita's exchange with Westerners was about *abstraction*, not Latin letters. Say "none found".
- **Western artists who trained in East Asian brush.** Mark Tobey (Teng Kuei, Seattle; Kyoto 1934); Fabienne Verdier (Huang Yuan, Sichuan, from 1984). Their results are abstraction ("white writing"; large abstracted gestures), not lettering. **Kazuaki Tanahashi** has written English words with the brush (the book titles *Brush Mind*, *Enso*); I did not find specimens. `[unverified]`
- **Net:** no tradition of "sumi Latin capitals" exists to lean on. A page that claims one is pastiche. The honest claim is smaller: *a Roman capital whose strokes are written in a brush's manner.*

**Typeface survey (rendered in this pass: "ARMSEBGW Ink", OFL fonts from google/fonts).**

| face | licence | what the Latin looks like | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kaushan Script | OFL | A slanted, commercial brush script, heavy, informal. | Reads as sign-painting. Too "brush font". |
| Caveat Brush | OFL | Marker-brush, rounded. | Reads as a marker. No. |

**Options for the drop:**

- **Option A (recommended): a Roman capital written by the brush.** Take a Roman inscriptional capital outline from an OFL face (EB Garamond cap, already approved in the pipeline; or Cinzel/Marcellus after licence check), extract the skeleton as `ductus.js` does, and write it with `letterWriter` using brush anatomy: **hidden-tip entries on stems and the horizontals; exposed entries on hairlines and diagonals; swell by `press(u)`; a pooled return at the foot of stems; a lift on the leg or tail with dry hair tracks at the finish.** The brush's own thick–thin replaces serifs; serifs become hidden-tip entries and returns (a real correspondence, per Catich). Stroke order follows the ductus (stem, bowl, hairlines last), which agrees with kaisho's top-to-bottom, left-to-right rule. Letter-by-letter notes: A (the left stroke exposes, the right swells, the bar light), M (two verticals, then the diagonals as one connected write), O (an ensō-like single stroke, see Option B), S, B, W. `[inferred]`. Size: 3 to 5 lines, per 03.
  - R-checks: letter from an OFL face (credited); brush effects generated; no dots (tracks only); written in order; dried state; dark mode: silver-white ink on indigo ground.
  - *Quality risk:* five failed procedural letters (illum-taste); keep the glyph as coverage and write the strokes as the deposit's *load*, not as a new letter. That is the approved route.
- **Option B: a capital beside or inside an ensō.** The circle is a single brush stroke, off-round and open, and the letter sits inside it or tucked against its open end. Works best for round letters (O, C, G, Q), where the letter would otherwise repeat the circle. The ensō is the *frame* and carries the rank; the letter is the content. Risk: the most clichéd composition; use it only for the highest rank (one per page, the opening), and never put a letter *over* a closed ensō like a logo. Also the letter must not be the "enlightenment" symbol; the ensō is the ornament, not a claim. R-checks as A.
- **Option C: a capital with a seal.** The letter in plain ink, with a small cinnabar seal pressed at its foot or shoulder, bearing a Latin monogram or a blank relief. It uses the real convention that a work is *finished by the seal*. Cheapest, and good for h3-level or the small drop. Risk: the seal must carry meaning (§4.7 and §7), and must not become a "chop" decoration.

**Not recommended:** a hand-built brushed alphabet; any face from the list above as-is; any faux-Asian stroke styling of Latin (Shojumaru-style terminals).

### 4.2 The paraph (¶ before a paragraph)

- **Option A: a single 点 (diǎn, "dot") stroke.** The first stroke of many characters, a short pressed-and-lifted stroke: press, a slight drag, a return or a tapered tail. It is a *brush mark*, with a tail and direction, not a round dot (so no conflict with the no-speckle rule); 0.4 to 0.7 lh, set in cinnabar or ink. It is one of the "eight principles of the character 永", a calligraphy teaching device, and the first stroke taught `[unverified]`. Gains: tiny, quick (low budget), crafted. Risk: reads as a bullet; vary shape per seed, never repeat.
- **Option B: a seal mini-impression**, a small cinnabar square (0.5 lh) with a blank or geometric relief, set in the margin. Real convention (head seals). Use sparingly (one per section), or it becomes a stamp-pad cliché.
- **Option C: a small ensō** (0.6 lh), reserved for the strongest paragraph breaks. Low-rank use cheapens the form; avoid except as the "sense break" mark in a text that is about stillness.

Recommendation: A for most, B for the first paraph of a major section.

### 4.3 The filler (the free end of a paragraph's last line)

- **Option A (recommended): a dry-brush trailing stroke.** One horizontal or gently rising stroke written *into the gap* from the text, 0.3 to 0.5 lh thick, beginning exposed (from the text's end) or hidden-tip (from the gap's start), with its load calibrated so it **runs dry exactly at the gap's end**: the free length determines the stroke's `Q0`, so the physical cause of the length is the brush's reservoir. Hair tracks open in the last third. Real basis: flying white at stroke finishes (Cai Jing's *Gongshi tie* commentary); the calligrapher's fast linking strokes. R: continuous tracks, no tooth-skipping dots; tail only grows.
- **Option B: a thinning 点-and-sweep** (a short pressed start and a long lift), for short gaps (0.5 to 2 em).
- **Option C: a single 一-like horizontal** (a flat horizontal stroke, ends with a hidden return), for medium gaps; sets as a rule. Note the stroke is a stroke, not the character "one", but it will be read that way; acceptable, even apt.
- Rule: no filler below 0.6 em (nothing real to write), and none above 12 em (a filler that long is a rule).

### 4.4 The heading run-out

- **Option A (recommended): a long horizontal brush line from the heading's end to the margin**, a single stroke written left to right, hidden-tip start close to the last letter, load depleting so the line thins and splits into hair tracks toward the margin and ends in a lift. It looks like the long horizontal *héng* of kaisho carried out far. h1 heavy (0.25 lh), h2 mid, h3 fine.
- **Option B: a stroke with a seal at the end**, for h1 only: the line ends at the margin and a small seal sits above it.
- **Option C: a bamboo-culm segment** (§4.5) running into the margin, for headings that sit at a section opening in nature-themed texts. Visual complexity is higher; keep to a single node.

### 4.5 Section breaks (three ranks)

- **Small break: one 点 stroke or a short horizontal dry-brush flick**, centred, 0.6 lh, the smallest possible gesture. Alternative: a paired short stroke, a "return" (a small curved flick) set off-centre, never symmetrical.
- **Major break: a bamboo stalk lying across the measure.** Bamboo is the classic first exercise of the four gentlemen: the culm is drawn in segments, each one stroke, with the stroke ending and a small gap at each node, and the node marked by a tapered "cap" stroke at the top of the segment and a pooled lower end `[unverified]`; branches and leaves come after. Cited works: Wu Zhen *Bamboo, old tree, and rock* (Met 39547, dated 1338, CC0), Xia Chang *Windblown bamboo* (Met 44590), Shitao *Bamboo in wind and rain* (49172), Zhao Mengfu (CMA 1963.515), Zheng Xie (Met 49244). A single horizontal bamboo segment of 2 to 3 nodes with 3 to 5 leaf strokes is feasible and real. Leaf strokes are a fixed vocabulary taught in the *Jieziyuan huazhuan* (1679); do not invent a leaf grammar, read the manual. **Not** characters: the bamboo-leaf cluster tradition refers to a particular stroke group (named "个" in some sources because it looks like the character); do not draw or label it as a character.
- **End break (the highest): an ensō with a seal.** One per document; the ensō is off-round, open, with a dry exit, the seal beneath in cinnabar, small. It is the "completion" mark: the page is signed (credo 12). Cited: Nantenbō (SF 2012.74), Sengai, Hakuin for ink anatomy. Do not use anywhere else on a page of reading text.
- **Alternate end break (a single gesture):** one long diagonal sweep with flying white in the tail, set off-axis, in the spirit of Kline's single black bar and Morita's one-stroke works (link only; copyright). The "gesture" ranks above the small break but below the ensō, so it can be the major break when bamboo reads too illustrative.
- Rank rule: small = a point, major = a stalk or a sweep, end = the ensō. Never decorate a "Subscribe" or footer.

### 4.6 The frames (quote, figure, code, pull quote, nav)

Real model: the *mounting* of a hanging scroll (表装 hyōsō), the narrow bands of silk or paper around a painting, and the ruled stationery paper. Frames stay quiet (house law 1).

- **Blockquote: a single vertical brush stroke in the left margin**, one pressed start, a long thinning stroke with flying white at the foot, the quote set against it (the margin-rule of the copyist). 0.15 to 0.25 lh thick.
- **Figure: a brush-drawn frame of four independent strokes** with *no corners joined* (each side a separate hidden-tip stroke, overshooting or short at the ends, like a hand-ruled line on a letter); or a *mounting band* in one slightly different paper tone. The mounting analogy is real; the wide mount borders of Japanese scrolls (the stripes above and below, the "ichimonji" band) are a design reference `[unverified]`.
- **Code: ruled lines** (烏絲欄 wūsī lán, "black-silk ruled columns" `[unverified]` term) as real stationery ruling; very pale 淡 lines. Note: the ruled-column layout is used in sutra paper; take it as stationery, not as sutra, and avoid 17-character grids.
- **Pull quote: a seal at one corner plus a brush rule**, for the editorially strongest element on a page; at most one per screen.
- **Nav: a row of 点-strokes** as markers (the current one heavier), or a dry-brush underline under the current item. Keep nav legible; no cultural gestures.
- R: frames use the same brush engine, no textured fills; dark mode uses silver ink on the ground (not gold except one seal-sized touch).

### 4.7 The seal, specifically

- **What it carries.** A seal's job is *authorship*. In illum, the seal belongs to the **colophon** (credo 12: maker, tools, seed, date, dedication). Its design options: (1) **a Latin monogram** (the maker's initials) in a carved square, a baiwen/zhuwen choice made per seed; (2) **a geometric or blank relief** (a diagonal bar, a framed square, a small circle), meaning nothing; (3) **the seed's glyph**, a hash-derived geometric pattern.
- **Never** faux seal script, "Chinese-looking" glyphs, or a translated name. If a real seal-script name were wanted, it would be commissioned from a seal carver, with the owner's own name carved correctly, and that is outside this library.
- **Impression:** square or rectangle (a tall rectangle is common for hand signatures), 0.5 to 1.2 lh, cinnabar. A seal in the final render is "pressed" with a **rolling contact**, the edge first, a pressure map across the face, so it *grows* from one corner across the face in 150–250 ms `[inferred]`: it is the same physics as a real rock-and-press and obeys "grow, don't pop".
- **Placement:** at the *end* of the writing (after the ensō, below the last line); one per page; colophon only. A seal on every heading is a stamp-collection cliché.

### 5.3 Cinnabar seal red

- Reference vermilion is `#E34234` (the standard colour-table value; `[unverified]` here).
- Real impressions vary a great deal with paste, load and paper: [measured] Zheng Xie's seals median `#D37562` (thin, aged, photographed).
- Recommendation: **`#C93A27`** as the base cinnabar (slightly cool, slightly deep, to sit with a warm grey ink), with the impression's density varying between about `#B5301F` (full pressure, fresh) and `#D8826F` (thin, light pressure). `#C93A27` is an estimate `[inferred]`; the owner's `--rubric` token wins.
- Cinnabar is *opaque* and sits over the ink; the seal and ink do not mix.

### 5.4 Dark mode: designed, not inverted

What exists:
- **Konshi (紺紙), indigo-dyed paper with gold and silver ink**, a Heian and Kamakura sutra tradition (§1.3). Measured from the Met photographs in this pass: ground `#2A2E39` (36104), `#2F2D2F` (45624), `#3A3C49` (829378); gold lines about `#DBBB69` (829378), `#AA884D` (36104), `#C3A06B` (45624). Silver on konshi tarnishes toward grey-black over time (silver sulphide) `[unverified]`, so surviving silver reads dim; fresh silver would be a bright grey-white.
- **Black-ground, white or metallic ink**: Morita's *Dragon Knows Dragon* (a black ground with gold-looking metallic paint); Tobey's white writing (white strokes over dark).
- **Gofun (胡粉) white**, ground oyster-shell calcium carbonate pigment, used for white lines in Japanese painting; the Met lists Shinoda's *Golden Tablets* (ink and gofun on gilded paper).

Proposed (all `[inferred]` as palette, tied to the above):

| role | hex | note |
|---|---|---|
| Night ground (konshi-like) | `#1E212B` to `#2A2E39` | a blue-black, not neutral; the measured konshi ground is lighter and greyer in photographs |
| Ink, the five tones inverted in *meaning* | 焦→`#F2EFE8` (white-silver, the strongest), 濃→`#D5D6DB`, 重→`#A9ABB5`, 淡→`#6F727E`, 清→`#454856` | silver-white ink on indigo: what Tobey and the konshi silver do. Tones remain a ladder, not a negation |
| Gold | `#DBBB69`, used **only at seal scale** (the owner: "gold only small") | the one small gold touch (the end seal's relief or the ensō's lowest stroke) |
| Seal in dark mode | the cinnabar `#C93A27` is *darker* than a pale ground, so lift it: `#D8503C` and keep it a flat impression | seals on dark ground exist in the form of red-on-black rarely; `[unverified]` |

**Light physics:** on a dark ground, silver ink *adds* light, not absorbs: the Kubelka–Munk model for the ink becomes additive over the ground, so a thin stroke is *faint*, not grey; the halo (nijimi) is a darker-indigo-to-lighter gradient, not a paler fringe. The engine's "dark mode as re-inking" is the right frame (04-web). The measured konshi photographs vary widely with lighting; calibrate against a raking-light capture before sign-off.

## 7. Risks

### 7.1 Cultural care

- **What is sacred or religious.**
  - **Sutra copying (写経 shakyō)** is a devotional practice (and a merit-making act) in Japan and China; the Chūsonji-kyō (1175) was a temple commission. The *surface* (dark indigo ground, silver and gold ink) is a visual tradition; the *layout* (ruled columns of a fixed character count, the text of the sutra) is devotional. Use the surface in dark mode; do not reproduce a sutra layout, do not set a sutra text, do not call the dark mode "sutra" in the UI. The documentation should say where the idea comes from and name the Chūsonji-kyō as the reference.
  - **Temple calligraphy and goshuin (御朱印)**, the red seal-and-brush records given at temples and shrines, are pilgrimage records. Do not imitate the format.
  - **The ensō** is a Zen teaching device; Zen teachers (Suzuki, Tanahashi) treat it as the maker's state on paper. Use it as a *form* of closure at the end of a page; do not claim meaning for it ("enlightenment") in the library's documentation.
  - **Seals** are personal identity; a seal means "this is mine"; a Latin monogram seal is honest, a carved "seal-script name" borrowed from a stock image is not.
- **What is appropriate as ornament.** Brush strokes as strokes (dots, horizontals, sweeps); bamboo, orchid and plum as natural subjects (a living, secular literati genre with open-access examples); the cinnabar seal as an authorship mark; dry-brush as a physical effect; ink tones; the mounting band as a frame idea. These are *techniques and conventions*, not sacred acts. The ensō and the konshi surface are the two to handle with care, and to credit.
- **Never put characters on the page.** The page's letters are Latin; no "Asian-looking" glyphs, no pseudo-kanji, no decorative CJK, no fake seal script, no "kanji tattoo" effect. Any real character used must be meaningful, correct, written by a trained hand or a licensed font with correct stroke order, and glossed. **v1 has none.** (The terms in this document are for the design team, not the page.)
- **Specifically avoid:** a "Zen" label on the page; a brush stroke used as a divider on a wellness-style page; red circles that look like the Japanese flag (a flat `#C93A27` disc reads as the Hinomaru; the ensō in ink, not cinnabar, and the seal square, never a red disc); sumi-e "Asian restaurant" menus; fake bamboo with a gradient; faux-calligraphy brush fonts for body or headings (Shojumaru-style "chop suey" faces); ensō or bamboo used as a *brand* motif.

### 7.2 How serious contemporary designers avoid pastiche

Evidence from the sources found is thin (no design essay was read in this pass). The working principles, from the research and from illum's own house record, are:
1. **Specific sources over a vague "East Asian look"** (name the work, the hand, the object; the CC0 images are the primary evidence).
2. **Translation of mechanics, not of look**: the brush's reservoir and hair tracks are what is borrowed, not Chinese or Japanese motifs.
3. **Restraint and negative space as the main content**, as Kline and the Bokujinkai did (white as shape); a page of dense "Asian" ornament is the opposite of the tradition.
4. **Credit and context** (a colophon naming the references, the licence of every image consulted).
5. **No religious claim** and no iconography taken for effect.
6. **Review by someone from the tradition** before release `[recommendation, not done]`.
Michaux's *Ideograms* and Tobey are both cautionary and instructive; I could not read a critical source this pass, so I flag the point rather than state a verdict.

### 7.4 Digital tells in brush simulation (the likely failures)

1. **Uniform hair streaks**: evenly spaced, equal-width, parallel lines running the whole stroke (a comb). Real tracks vary in width, merge, and die at different places.
2. **Perfect circles** (§3.3) and perfectly constant ensō width.
3. **Symmetric tapers**: the same point at both ends. Real strokes have a loaded start and a different, dry finish.
4. **Noise-grain inside the stroke** (the speckle the owner rejected); a texture overlay on the paper (rejected as "toy-level, extremely digital").
5. **Alpha-blended ink**: ink is absorbing; use the K–M layer and `exp(−OD)`, never alpha-over (02-analog).
6. **A uniform halo** of constant width around every stroke: the halo depends on dwell and speed (§2.6).
7. **Start and end blobs on every stroke** (every stroke pooled like a bullet): pooling happens at slow points and hidden-tip entries, not everywhere.
8. **The same stroke repeated** (a 点 stamped identically); seeded per element.
9. **Jitter** (per-point noise on the path) instead of hand physics: use the damped-spring pen model in `medium.js`.
10. **A blurred edge instead of a feathered one**: a Gaussian blur is isotropic; paper wicking follows fibre direction (§2.6).
11. **Gold everywhere** in dark mode (rejected "gold only small").
12. **Illegibility:** hair tracks and halos near text reduce contrast. The ornament must not cross a word (credo 6); tracks at 淡 or 清 tone only in the text's free gaps; test on the five-minute protocol (05-the-bar).

### The brush (sumi-brush.js)

A stroke is a path, a width at full pressure, a pressure profile, an entry (hidden: a blunt, slanted, pooled head; exposed: from a point; square: a flat brush set straight down) and an exit (lift, return, stop), a charge (where the middle hair runs dry), water (the halo), tone and pace. The hand is a minimum-jerk speed bell, slowed in tight turns (v ∝ R^⅓); ink is given per unit time, so heads and turns pool. The width is a set of tracks (unequal: many fine, a few broad clumps; the outer and the fine hold less). Each track spends its charge with the ink it lays and, once spent, narrows and ends once; a gap left by a spent hair may close as its neighbours spread; when most hairs are spent the last give out together (no lone hair runs on). The halo is a two-step fringe outside the live edge, width ∝ √(dwell) × wetness × the share of hairs still wet, with a slow variation along the stroke (never one width). A slow unevenness (±6 %) runs along the wet body. **The engine's dryness is always 0**, so its tooth-catching never fires: no speck, pinhole or island is ever made. Flat brushes (flying white, C) are the same tracks laid along a fixed nib angle. Every sweep carries its time, so the writer lays a stroke along its path at the brush's pace; the halo grows with the stroke (the dried state, never a fade). The seal is a pour: a carved face (knife wander on the edge), a pressure plane with slow curvature, a slow paste streak, rolling on from its fullest corner in ~240 ms. Latin initials (Cinzel, OFL) only.

### The directions

- **A · the written stroke (kaisho).** Round brush, sumi, separate strokes. Drop: Cinzel's capital poured as one sumi deposit (rim pooling, slow unevenness, an uneven halo), written along its skeleton. Paraph: a point in vermilion (shu, the teacher's red ink). Filler and run-out: one horizontal each, its charge spent where the gap ends. Breaks: a point; a long sweep off the axis; the ensō (once) and the seal. Quote: one vertical, a hanging needle.
- **B · the orchid leaf (after Zheng Xie).** Leaves only, one quick stroke each (swell, pinch, swell, long lift), two tones of one ink. Drop: the capital with two pale leaves rising behind it from its foot and crossing once. Filler and run-out: one long leaf each. Breaks: a leaf; three from one root (the phoenix eye); a clump and the seal. Quote: one leaf up its margin.
- **C · flying white (hihaku).** A flat brush at one angle, a light load, quick. Drop: the capital kept as paper in a block of three broad strokes (baiwen, written). Filler and run-out: one flat stroke, drying. Breaks: a short broad stroke; a long diagonal gesture; a broad circle (as Yoshihara Jirō's) and the seal, gold on the dark ground at seal size only.

Dark mode: the "inkstone" ground (#1b1d24, a blue-black slate, deliberately not the measured konshi greys of 2A2E39–3A3C49 and named for the ink's own stone, not for sutra paper), silver-white ink (233 231 224) whose tones are a ladder of light, the cinnabar lifted to 222 92 68. - **Fillers and run-outs** are B's leaf laid low. The belly is flattened (`swell` .7). The rise is held to half the x-height above the root and less below it (`flatY`). Checked at 19 px and on a 390 px phone.
- **The end mark** is the ensō, the clump or the flat-brush circle, each with the seal. The seed chooses one; `rich` leans toward the ensō and the clump.
- **The drop** is chosen by the seed (about 65/35):
  - **written:** A's capital as ink. The glyph keeps its own shape, but each cell is assigned to its skeleton stroke. It is densest where each stroke set down and spends along the stroke. Narrow white slits (lenses) open near the ends of horizontal strokes only: on a stem they read as a split letter at page size. The outline is a little off true, the halo uneven, and it pours stroke by stroke.
  - **reserved:** C's capital kept as paper. The block is three flat strokes with slanted starts and staggered lengths, loaded, with a halo, drying into slits. It stands the drop's own height, with the letter at .74 of it.

Engine changes:
- **Dry brush:** fine hairs hold less and die first. A drying clump keeps its width, then ends over a short way. Gaps close more often. The result is slits in a black body (Bankei) rather than thin parted lines.
- **Flat brush:** the edge never flips. A thin core marks where the stroke runs along the edge. Overlap and halo are normalised by the edge's length. Together these removed a sawtooth.
- **The seal:** its initial is now EB Garamond 500, lighter than Cinzel 700.

Speed, still ornament, first panel: 100 ms at dpr 1, 133 ms at dpr 2.

## Stage 2d: two drop directions (tests/sumi-drops.html, kit/styles/sumi-drops.js)

After the owner turned down every brushed Latin letter (the seven calligraphy faces, the authored alphabet, poured Cinzel, the reserved block), two directions are shown side by side, light and dark, in a paragraph, with A B M O S W V Y:

1. **The seal-cut capital.** The letter is cut from an OFL face into a stone (the knife's slow wander on every edge) and printed in cinnabar through the engine. It is the same `sealMask` as the small seals, with `worn` added. The border is chipped by two scales of slow noise; both are continuous, so there are no specks. The paste is starved toward the corners, and the press is a tilted plane with a slow curvature.
   - Both kinds are shown: baiwen (the letter white in red) and zhuwen (the letter red inside a red border).
   - The faces are Cinzel, EB Garamond and Archivo (wide black).
   - One size larger (1.3×) hangs up and out into the margin, never down over the fifth line.
2. **The type capital with one brush gesture.** The letter is the page's own type, crisp. One stroke from the same brush sits behind it:
   - an open arc (the ensō's stroke, pale, about 240°);
   - a broad flat dry stroke through a vermilion Cinzel capital;
   - a pale wet wash.

   An arc on the page means the end mark must not be the ensō (at most one a page).

Engine fix found here: a stroke's entry point and lift could taper over more than its own length, which made every short broad stroke a thin blade. Each taper is now capped at a share of the length (30 % and 45 %).

Speed: each drop takes 2–10 ms once the engine is compiled; the first takes 50–65 ms.

## Stage 2e: the seal drop, approved

The owner approved the Cinzel baiwen seal as the drop. The other directions are dropped; their files stay for the record. The drop is in `kit/styles/sumi.js`, together with A's hand, B's leaf fillers (a touch more belly: width .17 lh, swell .82) and the three end marks.

- **The stone** (`sealMask`, `worn`):
  - straight sides, each corner a hair off square;
  - one corner knocked off (sometimes two);
  - two or three small sharp chips, chosen by the seed.
- **The press:** a tilt, a slow curvature and a broad unevenness. The paste is starved toward one side or one corner, with one faint lighter patch. All of it is continuous.
- **The letter:** the knife's slow wander on the cut edges. Thin cuts are opened about 1 device px so a serif survives a small print.
- **The end-mark seals** come from the same carver: Cinzel cut, the same cinnabar (the circle's gold is gone) and the same edge.
- **Richness** leans the end mark: at 0 the circle is likeliest; at 1 the circle is never chosen. The leisure seal was skipped, because a small second stone beside the drop crowds it and its "simple mark" read as an icon.
- **Speed:** a whole page's still ornament takes 90 ms at dpr 1 (first panel, compiling) and 10–19 ms after; at dpr 2, 94 ms first and 14–37 ms after.

## Stage 3: shipped (kit/styles/sumi.js, the site's Sumi)

The owner approved the direction as it stands: A's hand, B's leaves, the three end marks and the seal drop. It ships as the written style `sumi`.

- **Code.** `kit/styles/sumi.js` is the rubricate interface. `sumi-pieces.js` holds the approved marks and imports `sumi-kaisho`, `sumi-ran` and `sumi-hihaku`. `sumi-brush.js` is the brush, the seal and the printer, and `sumi-letter.js` the glyph masks.
  - Shipped faces: only the Cinzel subset, `fonts/sumi/illum-sumi-cinzel-caps.woff2`, with its note and the OFL.
  - Not shipped: the studies `sumi-alphabet.js`, `sumi-drops.js`, `tests/sumi-letters.html`, `sumi-drops.html` and `sumi-faces.html`, and the candidate CJK subsets in `fonts/sumi/cand/`. They stay in the working copy, uncommitted.
- **Seeds.** Every piece's seed is moved by the page's, so each page has its own:
  - the seal's lean, chips, knocked corner, starved side and lighter patch;
  - the end mark;
  - the leaves' belly, lean, tone and arch;
  - the brush's pressure wander, pace, wetness and the point where each stroke runs dry.

  Each page stays the same for its seed.
- **The budget**, from plain to lavish:

  | level | adds |
  |---|---|
  | plain | the seal drop, the paraph, the run-outs, a point, a sweep, and the quiet circle at the end |
  | modest | the paragraph fillers, the quotation's vertical, the points at the contents |
  | usual | the aside's and code's verticals, the pull quote's horizontal, the figure's horizontal under its caption |
  | fine | a second, paler leaf in run-outs, and a horizontal over the figure |
  | lavish | the drop's seal a size larger, hung up and out where the page leaves room; a second leaf in fillers; a seal at the chapter sweep's foot; the pull quote's horizontal under it too; the end mark never the circle |

  The leisure seal and brushed Latin letters are out.
- **Drawn.** Each piece's still is revealed through its own path, the brush's sweeps at their own moments and widths (`inkletter.js` sweepIn 'reveal'). Each seal is revealed by a soft front from its fullest corner. No piece comes in whole.
- **Speed.** These figures were measured with the machine heavily loaded (load average 47–66 from other agents' browsers and builds), so they are noisy.
  - On the built site's see page, the style's own pieces (13 at plain, 32–35 above) take 140–200 ms in all at dpr 2 at plain, and 210–490 ms at usual and lavish. The first piece pays for the WebGL context, the shader compile and the JIT: 50–150 ms, and up to 300 ms in the slowest runs.
  - Every later piece takes 1–15 ms.
  - On the specimen with the dev server warm: 33–35 pieces in 70–300 ms.
  - Changes made for this: a page's widest piece fits the first sheet, and the sheet grows by half when it must grow. Brush steps are a device px or two. The seal's slow fields are read on a 4 px grid, and its inner loop skips paper. The still's extra flow step is gone.
  - Not done: printing a page's pieces on one sheet with one render and read-back per ink, as Typewriter does. That is the next step if the see page must sit under 200 ms everywhere.
- **Checks.**
  - `tests/sumi.check.mjs` passes: 24 specimen pages and 24 built see pages (1440 and 390, light and dark, rich 0, .5 and 1, seeds 1 and 7) with no ink on a word and none cut off. Cold loads of the built see page pass (12 of 12, drawn live: sumi is not in the bake's default). The drawn pages pass at four budgets, with no piece coming in whole.
  - Every other style's check passes, at reduced settings: blackletter, insular-knot, occult, tarot, astrology, pie-rock, rock, riso, typewriter and plotter.
- **Swatches.** `site/swatches/sumi-{light,dark}.webp`, drawn with `tools/swatches.mjs` on the built site.
- **Stage 3b: a page's pieces on one sheet** (commit after b07840c).
  - `sumi-brush.js` printMany puts each piece in its own cell of one sheet and pours ink by ink: one pour, one render and one copy out of the GPU per ink. `sumi.js` batches a page's still pieces, as Plotter and Typewriter do.
  - The brush's planning costs a third less CPU (CPU time, 8 repeats of 22 pieces at dpr 2: 580–690 ms before, 450–470 ms after). The sweeps are the same to the byte (sha256 of every record identical).
  - Output against the previous commit (`tools/diff.mjs`, dpr 1 and 2, 1440 and 390, light and dark): at most 2 levels in 255 on tens to a few hundred pixels. The previous commit shows the same kind of difference against itself between two loads (47 px, at most 2).
  - Checks: `sumi.check.mjs` passes on the specimen (24 pages), on the drawn page at four budgets (no piece whole), and on the built see page with 12 cold loads.
