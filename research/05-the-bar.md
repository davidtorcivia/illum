# 05: The Bar

**What "good enough for the permanent collection of a major institution" means, and how to score it in five minutes.**

## 0. The rubric on one page (use this; detail in §3)

Score each criterion 0–3. **Beauty is weighted ×3 and ranks first.** When comparing candidates, sort by the Beauty score first. The total only breaks ties within the same Beauty score. A conceptually brilliant piece that scores Beauty 2 can therefore never outrank a plain thing that scores Beauty 3. This is the user's directive, built into the arithmetic.

| # | Criterion | Weight | The test (≤30 s each) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Beauty**: the immediate aesthetic response | ×3 | Cold-glance, squint and grayscale tests |
| 2 | **Craft at every scale** | ×1 | Zoom test (400%) and thumbnail test (25%) |
| 3 | **Holds at every viewport** | ×1 | Small-screen test (390 px) |
| 4 | **Integration with the text** | ×1 | Remove-it test and swap-the-text test |
| 5 | **Behaviour** | ×1 | Poke test (resize, hover, scroll, reduced-motion) |
| 6 | **The system is the work** | ×1 | 100-seeds test |
| 7 | **Living imperfection** | ×1 | Ruskin test (where is the hand?) |
| 8 | **Restraint and the void** | ×1 | One-thing-less test |
| 9 | **History without pastiche** | ×1 | Pastiche test |
| 10 | **Longevity** | ×1 | Date test |
| 11 | **Point of view** | ×1 | One-sentence test |
| 12 | **Provenance and legibility of the system** | ×1 | Accession test |

**Maximum score: 42** (Beauty 9 + 11 × 3).

- **Museum:** Beauty = 3, no criterion below 2, and total ≥ 36.
- **Good work, not yet museum:** Beauty ≥ 2 and total ≥ 28.
- **Demo:** anything with Beauty ≤ 1, whatever its total. Rework it.

**Five-minute protocol:**

1. Open the page at 1440 px and look for 2 seconds before reading anything. Score Beauty provisionally.
2. Zoom in to 400% on a corner. Then zoom out to 25%.
3. Resize to 390 px.
4. Hide the ornament (toggle the ornament layer in devtools, or with the library's off switch once it has one). Restore it, then change the heading text.
5. Open the 100-seed contact sheet.
6. Read the README and the one-sentence statement.
7. Revisit the Beauty score last. You may lower it after the other tests. You may never raise it because of a concept.

## 1. Evidence: what institutions acquired and the words they used

### 1.1 MoMA, Architecture & Design

**Video games, 2012.** In November 2012 MoMA announced 14 initial games, including *Pac-Man*, *Tetris*, *Myst*, *SimCity 2000*, *Portal*, *flOw*, *Dwarf Fortress*, *Passage*, *Katamari Damacy* and *Vib-Ribbon* (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_video_games_in_the_Museum_of_Modern_Art `[fetched]`). Antonelli's original post (https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2012/11/29/video-games-14-in-the-collection-for-starters/) is `[403]`.

- **The four criteria.** "Behavior, aesthetics, space, and time" is how the press and Wikipedia summarize them. It is a summary, not a verbatim Antonelli sentence (`[fetched]`, Wikipedia; GamesBeat says the same).
- **Antonelli verbatim:**
  - "The real core of interactive design is behavior."
  - Designers "design behaviors that go on to influence the rest of our lives."
  - She calls games "the purest form of interaction design… unadulterated by any sort of function or finality."
  - Source: https://gamesbeat.com/why-curator-paola-antonelli-added-video-games-to-the-museum-of-modern-art/ `[fetched]`.
- **The headline line:** "Are video games art? They sure are, but they are also design." She sought games that were a "successful synthesis of materials and techniques." Source: https://hyperallergic.com/one-up-moma-acquires-its-first-classic-video-games-as-art `[fetched]`.
- **What is collected:** "when we cannot acquire the code, we acquire simulations, emulations, the cartridge, the hardware," and "what we're interested in showing is the interaction by itself." There was also an exclusion rule: "no gratuitous violence." Source: Wikipedia `[fetched]`.
- **Code elegance.** One secondary account says the criteria also weighed "the elegance of the code and the design of the player's behavior" (`[search]`).

**The @ symbol, 2010.** Antonelli's post of 22 March 2010 (https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2010/03/22/moma-acquires-the-symbol/) is `[403]`. As quoted by a secondary source, the acquisition "relies on the assumption that physical possession of an object as a requirement for an acquisition is no longer necessary." A second source has her saying: "We don't need to possess them. We can still show them." The rationale was the sign's shift in use and meaning after Ray Tomlinson chose it for email in 1971 (`[search]`, via Bloomberg and SVA blog coverage).

*Lesson for illum:* the thing acquired can be a **rule or a convention**, not an object.

**23 digital typefaces, January 2011.** The set included OCR-A, Verdana and Walker by Matthew Carter, and others. It was shown in *Standard Deviations* (2011–12). Before this the only typeface in the collection was 36-pt Helvetica Bold lead type. Helvetica was not part of the 23. Antonelli called the faces "sublimely elegant responses to the issues of specific media" and "a new branch in our collection tree." Primary: https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2011/01/24/moma-acquires-23-digital-typefaces-for-its-collection/ `[403]`; quotes via https://subtraction.com/2011/01/24/museum-of-modern-art-acquires-23-new-typefaces-for-permanent-collection `[search]`.

*Lesson:* **fitness to the medium is the criterion.** Verdana is in the collection because it was designed for the screen pixel, not in spite of that.

**Refik Anadol, *Unsupervised – Machine Hallucinations*, October 2023.** It was a gift from donors, and MoMA called it its first tokenized work in the permanent collection. It was acquired with Ian Cheng's *3FACE*. There is no published curatorial rationale in the sources found (https://www.artnews.com/art-news/artists/moma-acquires-refik-anadol-unsupervised-digital-art-nfts-1234681622 `[search]`).

*Caution:* the acquisition was gifted and the work spectacular, but the rationale was never made public. It is not a model for this rubric.

**Chromie Squiggles (Snowfro) ×8, a complete set of the eight types, and CryptoPunks (Larva Labs) ×8, December 2025.** These went to Media & Performance as donations. In the same month MoMA took Rafaël Rozendaal's website work *Shape Squeeze* (2018) and six on-chain works (https://www.rightclicksave.com/article/digital-art-acquisitions-in-2025-moma-whitney-centre-pompidou `[fetched]`). Rozendaal said: "Museums exist to separate art from chaos" (`[fetched]`, same article).

*Lesson:* MoMA took **the complete set of Squiggle types**. The collected unit of a generative work is the system's range, not one output.

### 1.2 Cooper Hewitt: *Planetary* (Bloom Studio), 2013

This was the museum's first acquisition of code. From "Planetary: collecting and preserving code as a living object," https://www.cooperhewitt.org/2013/08/26/planetary-collecting-and-preserving-code-as-a-living-object/ `[fetched]`:

- Acquisition "does not and should not seal them in carbonite like Han Solo."
- "Open sourcing the code is akin to a panda breeding program"; the work "will be 'experienced' through its offspring."
- They collected the source "including its changes between versions," with "all the documentation of bugs, feature additions, and code changes throughout Planetary's life."
- Released under BSD. Tom Carden agreed to be "final arbiter" of fixes.
- A copy was printed on archival paper as a "master."
- The aim is to "preserve not only the form of the things in their collections, but their creator's intent."

The museum's justification page called it a chance to lead in collecting "living software" (`[search]`; https://collection.cooperhewitt.org/objects/35520989/justification now redirects).

*Lesson:* **the version history, the intent and the licence are part of the object.**

The National Design Awards criteria were historically "excellence, innovation, and enhancement of the quality of life," and are now "innovation and impact." An Interaction Design category was added in 2009 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Design_Awards `[search]`).

### 1.3 Whitney: Harold Cohen, *AARON* (2024 show; software acquired 2023)

From https://whitney.org/press/harold-cohen-aaron `[fetched]`:

- AARON is "the earliest artificial intelligence software for artmaking" and "one of the longest-running contemporary art projects."
- The Whitney is "the first and only museum to collect versions of the AARON software from different time periods."

Christiane Paul (curator) has stressed in coverage that **the software is the artwork**, distinct from its outputs (plots, paintings, screen images). She called AARON KCAT (2001), acquired in 2023, "one of those holy grails of digital art history in the collection" (`[search]`, via The Art Newspaper and Wallpaper coverage).

Artport (whitney.org/artport) has commissioned net art since 2001 `[unverified]`. Cory Arcangel's *Super Mario Clouds* and other works are in Whitney holdings `[unverified]`.

*Lesson:* **collect the generator, in several versions, with live demonstration.**

### 1.4 V&A: the computer-art collections

From "Computer art at the V&A," https://research.vam.ac.uk/journals/research-journal/issue-2/computer-art-at-the-v-and-a/ `[fetched]`:

- **Why the V&A collects it:** the policy covers works that "fall between fine art and design." The arrival of the computer is "one of the most culturally significant developments of the last century," and the aim is to "place it on the art historical map."
- **Process as well as object:** the museum also collects "printing tools and equipment" and documentation.
- **Nake:** *Hommage à Paul Klee* (1965) is "an excellent example of Nake's early exploration of Bense's theories."
- **Molnár:** *Letters from my Mother* (1988) "illustrates well the relationship between artistic intuition and a more objective control."

Holdings and how they arrived:

- **Patric Prince gift:** about 200 works plus archive.
- **Computer Arts Society archive:** about 250 works.
- **Purchases:** Molnár's *Interruptions* (1969) in 2011, and Nake in 2014 with Art Fund support.
- **Other artists:** Georg Nees, Harold Cohen, Manfred Mohr and Paul Brown are represented.

Sources: the article above, and https://digitalartarchive.siggraph.org/?p=32087 `[search]`.

**Molnár's "1% disorder."** Molnár's *1% de désordre* (1976) and her book *Ein Prozent Unordnung* (1980) argue for a small, systematic dose of chance inside order (`[search]`, https://spalterdigital.com/?p=6035). This is the most directly applicable principle in the whole history for illum.

**Rapid Response Collecting** (from 2014, led by Kieran Long). It acquires objects while they are current, such as the Liberator 3D-printed gun and Primark jeans, because "global events… have a bearing on the world of design and manufacturing." Primary Dezeen article `[403]`; summary via https://cdn.antiquestradegazette.com/news/2014/vanda-launch-a-rapid-response-to-contemporary-collecting `[search]`.

*Lesson:* objects can enter a collection as **evidence of a moment**. This is the opposite of illum's goal, which is to avoid being datable.

### 1.7 Illuminated manuscripts: what makes a masterpiece

- **Hours of Catherine of Cleves**, Morgan M.917/M.945, Utrecht, about 1440. Morgan director William Griswold called it "indisputably one of the greatest" of the Morgan's manuscripts, "among the most beautiful and sophisticated illuminated works ever created." Press release https://www.themorgan.org/press/2009/hours-catherine-cleves-exhibition `[403]`, quotes `[search]`.
  - Its fame rests on its **borders**: mussels, pretzels, coins and fishing nets that comment on the miniature they frame `[unverified]`, standard literature.
- **Spinola Hours**, Getty MS Ludwig IX 18, about 1510–20, Master of James IV of Scotland. Former Getty curator Thomas Kren called it "the most pictorially ambitious and original sixteenth-century Flemish manuscript." There is also a variant wording, so `[search]`.
  - **The mechanism of integration:** the borders host illusionistic flowers and birds that "appear to cast shadows on colored backgrounds," and scrolls appear "pinned to the pages of facing miniatures." The border and the page surface are both in play (`[search]`, facsimile-publisher descriptions).
- **Lindisfarne Gospels**, British Library Cotton MS Nero D IV, about 715–20, attributed to Eadfrith. It has five carpet pages and was "an artistic and technical innovator."
  - **The relevant detail:** he used a lead stylus to rough out designs on the back of the page, with "a compass and straight-edge for the geometric core." The geometry underneath is constructed; the knotwork and birds on top are freehand (https://smarthistory.org/the-lindisfarne-gospels `[search]`).

**What curators praise, synthesized across all three:**

1. **Invention within a convention.** The border type is old; what is put in it is new.
2. **Text and image on one surface.** The ornament comments on, frames and points at the text, and the text sits *in* the decoration.
3. **A constructed geometric underlayer, executed by hand on top.**
4. **Virtuosity visible at the closest viewing distance.**
5. **A specific patron and a specific occasion.** The book is *for* someone.

### 1.8 Critics and principles

- **Dieter Rams, ten principles** (https://www.vitsoe.com/us/about/good-design `[fetched]`). Relevant:
  - #3 "only well-executed objects can be beautiful."
  - #7 "It avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated."
  - #8 "Nothing must be arbitrary or left to chance."
  - #10 "Less, but better."
  - *Note the tension between #8 and Ruskin and Molnár.* It resolves this way: the chance must itself be *designed*. Chance in a designed place is fine; chance everywhere is arbitrary.
- **Owen Jones, *Grammar of Ornament*, 1856** (https://readingdesign.org/grammar-of-ornament `[fetched]`; numbering follows that transcription):
  - 5: "Construction should be decorated. Decoration should never be purposely constructed."
  - 6: "lines growing out of one from the other in gradual undulations."
  - 7: general forms first, then subdivision, then ornament enriched for close viewing.
  - 8: "All ornament should be based upon a geometrical construction."
  - 11: "In surface decoration all lines should flow out of a parent stem."
  - 12: "All junctions of curved lines… should be tangential."
  - 13: conventionalized, not literal, natural forms.
  - 36: "The principles discoverable in the works of the past belong to us; not so the results."
- **Ruskin, "The Nature of Gothic"** (*Stones of Venice* II; https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/ruskin/eSoV/texts/vol10/vol10p203.html `[fetched]`):
  - "Of human work none but what is bad can be perfect, in its own bad way."
  - "imperfection is in some sort essential to all that we know of life."
  - "Nothing that lives is, or can be, rigidly perfect; part of it is decaying, part nascent."
  - "to banish imperfection is to destroy expression, to [check exertion, to paralyze vitality]." The bracketed completion is `[search]`.
  - Savageness and Changefulness are the first two characteristics of the Gothic.
- **Sol LeWitt, "Paragraphs on Conceptual Art"** (*Artforum*, 1967): "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art" (`[search]`, https://gwern.net/doc/design/1967-lewitt.pdf).
- **Bringhurst**: "Typography exists to honor content" (the first principle, *Elements of Typographic Style* §1.1). Also: "The typographer's one essential task is to interpret and communicate the text" (`[search]`, https://www.viget.com/articles/typography-must-honor-content/).
- **Frank Chimero, *The Shape of Design*, ch. 2** (https://shapeofdesignbook.com/chapters/02-craft-and-beauty/ `[fetched]`):
  - "A polished axe does not chop better."
  - "Beauty is a special form of craft that goes beyond making something work better."
  - "Craft is a love letter from the work's maker."
  - The Shaker line: "if it is both [necessary and useful], do not hesitate to make it beautiful."
- **Craig Mod, "Books in the Age of the iPad" (2010).** Formless content reflows without loss; Definite content "is aware of the page" (https://craigmod.com/journal/ipad_and_books/ `[search]`).
  - *Implication:* illum makes otherwise Formless web text partly Definite, and must survive the reflow.
- **Robin Sloan, "An App Can Be a Home-Cooked Meal" (2020).** Software made for specific people need not scale; its value lies in its specificity (https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/home-cooked-app `[search]`). This parallels the manuscript principle that the book is made for a patron.
- **Ellen Lupton, *Thinking with Type*.** Not verified this session. Her general position, that typography is a visual interface to the text, is `[unverified]`.

## 2. Alexander's fifteen properties, applied to generated ornament

Source: *The Nature of Order*, Book 1, *The Phenomenon of Life* (2002). The names follow the canonical list, via the summary at https://www.jackcheng.com/sunday/384-fifteen-properties/ `[fetched]`.

Alexander warns that "the precise number fifteen is not significant." The properties are diagnostic, not a recipe. They describe relationships between **centres**: coherent zones that strengthen one another.

1. **Levels of Scale.** "Every order of magnitude is present."
   - *For illum:* the initial, the border band, the rule, the corner piece, the motif, the stroke and the stroke's texture are each designed, with roughly a 2–4× ratio between adjacent levels.
   - *Failure:* one scale of motif plus pixel noise, with nothing in between.
   - *Test:* zoom.
2. **Strong Centres.** Surrounding elements point toward a centre and intensify it.
   - *For illum:* every ornamental field has a focus, such as a corner piece, a knot or the initial. Line direction and density converge on it.
   - *Failure:* all-over texture with no place for the eye to land.
3. **Boundaries (thick).** Edges are at a scale similar to what they enclose.
   - *For illum:* the border band is a real zone, roughly 1/8 to 1/4 of the field it frames, with its own inner and outer rules. It is not a 1 px CSS border.
   - *Failure:* ornament floating with no frame, or a frame drawn as a hairline.
4. **Alternating Repetition.** The gaps between repeats form a second repeating motif.
   - *For illum:* in a running border, both the motif and the counter-space between motifs are drawn shapes, for example leaf, bud, leaf, bud.
   - *Failure:* identical stamps at a fixed interval.
5. **Positive Space.** Negative space is as well shaped as the figure, so nothing is left over.
   - *For illum:* the background shapes left between vines and the text block are convex, legible and deliberate.
   - *Failure:* random leftover slivers. Check with the inverse test: invert the ornament mask. Does the background read as shapes?
6. **Good Shape.** Each element is itself a good shape, recursively.
   - *For illum:* a single leaf, a single stroke and a single terminal each look good alone at 800%.
   - *Failure:* blobs made of blobs.
7. **Local Symmetries.** Many small symmetries inside an asymmetric whole.
   - *For illum:* each flower, knot or cartouche is near-symmetric. The page composition is not mirror-symmetric.
   - *Failure:* global mirror symmetry (clip-art), or no local symmetry at all (mush).
8. **Deep Interlock and Ambiguity.** Centres hook into their neighbours, and boundaries blur.
   - *For illum:* vines pass *behind* the initial's stem and *over* the border rule. The ornament reaches into the text block's margin, and the text's first word sits inside the ornament.
   - *Failure:* ornament and text in separate boxes.
9. **Contrast.** Differences that unify, like silence after a hand-clap.
   - *For illum:* dense against void, dark against light, gold against ink, straight against curved (Jones 10). There is a real value range, not mid-tones only.
   - *Failure:* everything at 40% opacity.
10. **Gradients.** Transitions that reflect the underlying forces.
    - *For illum:* density thins away from the centre. Strokes taper toward their tips. Ornament density responds to text density, so there is less near body copy and more in margins.
    - *Failure:* CSS linear gradients standing in for structure.
11. **Roughness.** Irregularity that comes from fitting the context, not from an imposed filter.
    - *For illum:* a motif bends to fit the actual space available. A leaf is clipped because a heading is there. Jitter comes from the drawing process, such as pen pressure and ink pooling at a stroke's end.
    - *Failure:* a uniform random offset applied to every vertex.
12. **Echoes.** The same angles and families of curve recur across scales.
    - *For illum:* the border's curve family, the initial's swash and the leaf shape share one generating curve. One "hand" generates all scales.
    - *Failure:* a motif library assembled from unrelated sources.
13. **The Void.** A calm, empty centre that gives the busy parts meaning.
    - *For illum:* every viewport has at least one generous ornament-free zone, usually the text block itself. The text block is the void.
    - *Failure:* ornament under or behind body text.
14. **Simplicity and Inner Calm.** Remove every centre that does not help. This is not minimalism.
    - *For illum:* after the one-thing-less test, nothing more can be removed without loss.
    - *Failure:* five effects layered because each one was easy to add.
15. **Not-Separateness.** The thing belongs to its surroundings; "you cannot really tell where one thing breaks off."
    - *For illum:* the ornament reads as part of *this* page's typography, palette and grid. Taken to another site it would look wrong.
    - *Failure:* a decoration that could be pasted onto any page.

## 3. The rubric

Each criterion has a 0–3 scale, a procedural test with a pass condition, and its sources.

### 1. Beauty (×3, ranks first)

*Sources:* user directive; Rams #3; Chimero; Jones 4 ("repose"); Griswold ("most beautiful").

Beauty is scored **before** reading any statement or code, and the score can only be lowered by later tests, never raised. Rate each of the four components below (colour, line, rhythm, proportion) from 0 to 3 against its bullets. **The Beauty score is the lowest of the four**, so one ugly component caps it.

- **Colour harmony.**
  - A limited palette (typically 2–5 inks plus paper) with one clear value structure.
  - It still reads in grayscale.
  - Saturated colour is used in small quantities against neutrals, the way manuscript gold and vermilion are rationed.
  - Colours do not muddy where they meet. Owen Jones has a colour proposition, numbered 13 in some transcriptions, that reads "Colours should never be allowed to impinge upon each other" `[search]`.
- **Line quality.**
  - Strokes have entry, body and exit, with pressure, taper and modulation tied to direction.
  - Curves meet tangentially (Jones 12).
  - There are no uniform-width vector strokes unless that monoline is the deliberate idiom, and no kinks, overshoots or stair-stepping.
- **Rhythm.** Intervals vary purposefully, like a measure, not a metronome and not white noise: long-short-long spacing, density pulses, a cadence along a border.
- **Proportion.**
  - The border width, motif size, margin and text measure relate by a simple unit (Jones 9).
  - The ornament's visual weight is matched to the page's type weight.

**Scale:**

- **0:** The first-glance response is neutral or negative. Muddy or arbitrary colours, uniform-width wobbly lines, even spacing, sizes picked by eye.
- **1:** Pleasant but generic, the "nice demo" response. One component is right, such as a good palette, but the lines are dead or the rhythm is mechanical.
- **2:** Clearly attractive. Colour and line are both good. A trained eye finds one weak component, typically rhythm or proportion.
- **3:** Arresting. The viewer leans in within 2 seconds, before knowing what it is. All four components hold, and the piece survives the grayscale and squint tests.

**Tests:**

- **Cold-glance test.** Show the page for 2 seconds to someone who has not been told what it is. Pass if they ask to look again.
- **Squint test.** Blur the screenshot by 8 px. Pass if a clear, pleasing shape of darks and lights remains, rather than an even grey.
- **Grayscale test.** Desaturate the screenshot. Pass if the hierarchy still holds and the ornament does not dissolve into the paper.

### 2. Craft at every scale

*Sources:* Alexander 1, 6; Rams #8; Jones 7; Lindisfarne.

- **0:** Designed at one scale only. At 400% you see raw polylines, noise speckle or JPEG mush. At 25% it turns grey.
- **1:** Two scales are designed (motif and layout), but strokes are monoline or the texture is a single noise layer.
- **2:** Three or more levels hold, with one dead level: usually stroke terminals, or the transition from border to motif.
- **3:** At 400%, any corner shows a decision at every level (motif, stroke, terminal, ink texture). At 25%, the thumbnail reads as one strong shape.

**Tests:**

- **Zoom test.** Take a 400% screenshot of three random corners. Pass if every element visible was clearly authored at that scale, and nothing is merely a by-product of the scale above.
- **Thumbnail test.** View at 25%. Pass if a clear silhouette and value structure remain.

### 3. Holds at every viewport

*Sources:* user directive (desktop-first, must work on mobile); Mod on reflow; Rhizome on restageability.

- **0:** On mobile the ornament is hidden with `display:none`, or simply scaled down until strokes become hairlines and motifs illegible. Or it overflows and causes horizontal scroll.
- **1:** It survives on mobile but is plainly a reduced desktop: same composition, cramped, with borders eating the measure.
- **2:** There is a deliberate mobile composition, for example the border becomes a head-and-tail band, but one element is awkward.
- **3:** At 390 px it is still beautiful and intentional, as if designed for that width first. Ornament re-composes rather than shrinks: fewer and larger centres, the void preserved, the text measure protected. Stroke weights are tuned for small-screen pixel density, there is no horizontal scroll, and touch does not trigger hover-only effects. Between 390 and 1440, every intermediate width looks designed and nothing jumps.

**Small-screen test.** Load the page at 390 px wide. Pass only if a viewer who had never seen the desktop version would judge it designed for the phone. Then drag the window slowly from 390 to 1440 px. Fail on any width where the ornament collides with the text, pops, or looks orphaned.

### 4. Integration with the text

*Sources:* Bringhurst; Alexander 8, 15; Cleves and Spinola borders; Jones 5.

- **0:** A background layer behind the content, with no relationship to the text. It could be removed and the page would be cleaner.
- **1:** It frames content in a generic way. The same border appears around every block regardless of what it holds.
- **2:** It responds to the structure of the text, such as heading level, section breaks and the first letter, but not to the meaning.
- **3:** The ornament and the text are one surface. It interlocks with the actual letterforms (the initial, rag edges, the measure). It is derived from the content: its length, structure, keywords or a chosen motif vocabulary. It points the eye into the reading. Removing it makes the page worse, not just plainer.

**Tests:**

- **Remove-it test.** Toggle the ornament off. Pass if the page is clearly worse: less legible hierarchy, less sense of entrance and of place. If the page is just as good, fail.
- **Swap-the-text test.** Change the heading and paragraph lengths. Pass if the ornament visibly re-fits.

### 5. Behaviour

*Sources:* Antonelli ("the real core of interactive design is behavior"); Rhizome ("acts on the network"); AARON shown drawing live.

- **0:** Static, or animated without reason: perpetual drift, parallax for its own sake. Ignores `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **1:** Responds to one input, typically hover, generically.
- **2:** Responds sensibly to layout changes (resize, re-flow, theme) and respects reduced motion. Its motion is tied to an event.
- **3:** Its behaviour expresses the idea: it grows as you read, is drawn on reveal with real stroke order, or settles on resize like ink. Its behaviour has a *character* that could be described the way Antonelli describes a game's. It is fully calm under reduced motion and still beautiful in its static state.

**Poke test.** Resize, scroll, hover, switch the theme, then toggle reduced motion, all within 60 seconds. Pass if every response is purposeful and nothing jitters, re-randomizes or janks.

### 6. The system is the work

*Sources:* LeWitt; Whitney and Paul (software as artwork, collected in versions); MoMA taking all eight Squiggle types; Autoglyphs ("realised through a protocol").

- **0:** One hand-tuned seed looks good. Other seeds look broken or identical.
- **1:** Seeds vary only in noise. They are recognisably the same image wobbled.
- **2:** Seeds vary meaningfully, but about 10% are weak or broken, or the variation drifts out of one hand.
- **3:** Every seed is exhibitable. The variation is structural (composition, motif choice, density). The whole set reads as one author. Some seeds are surprising and better than the curated default.

**100-seeds test.** Render a 10×10 contact sheet. Fail if:

- any two are indistinguishable at a glance;
- any one is broken (collision, overflow, empty field, muddy colour);
- the set does not read as one hand;
- you would not frame at least ten of them.

### 7. Living imperfection

*Sources:* Ruskin (Savageness, Changefulness); Molnár 1%; Alexander 11; Rams #8 as the counterweight.

- **0:** Either sterile perfection (vector-clean, identical repeats), or uniform random jitter applied to every vertex at one amplitude.
- **1:** Imperfection is a filter: wobble, grain or roughen applied after drawing.
- **2:** Imperfection comes from a process model, such as pen pressure, ink pooling or brush dryness, but it is uniform across the piece.
- **3:** Imperfection is *located* and *motivated*. Strokes start confident and end loose. The hand tires toward the end of a border. A motif squeezes because space ran out. Ink density varies along the stroke. The error budget is small and placed: Molnár's 1%, not 30%. Every irregularity can be explained by a cause.

**Ruskin test.** Point to three irregularities and give the cause of each. Fail if the answer is "random."

### 8. Restraint and the void

*Sources:* Rams #10; Alexander 13, 14; Jones 6 ("nothing superfluous").

- **0:** Ornament everywhere: behind text, on every card, on every hover. The page has no rest.
- **1:** It is bounded, but it is overdense, or several effects are stacked (grain, glow, shadow).
- **2:** It has a clear void and a sensible quantity. One element could go.
- **3:** Concentrated where it matters: openings, transitions, the initial, the colophon. Generous calm elsewhere. Nothing further can be removed without loss.

**One-thing-less test.** Remove the weakest single element. Pass if the piece gets worse. If it gets better, it was not finished.

### 9. History without pastiche

*Sources:* Jones 36 ("principles… belong to us; not so the results"); the Pompidou "genealogy"; Cleves (invention within a convention).

- **0:** A copy of a historical style, such as a fake Kells knot or stock Art Nouveau corners, or no awareness of tradition at all.
- **1:** Borrowed motifs with a digital finish: Celtic knots in neon.
- **2:** Historical *principles* applied (parent stem, carpet-page construction, strewn-flower borders) with recognizable sources.
- **3:** A knowledgeable viewer can name the lineage (Insular geometry, Ghent-Bruges trompe-l'œil, Morris, Jones) but not the source being copied. The principles are carried into something only the web and code can do, such as reflow, generation or response.

**Pastiche test.** Put the piece next to its closest historical source. Pass if it is clearly *of* the tradition and clearly *not* a reproduction, and if you can say what it adds that the source could not have.

### 10. Longevity

*Sources:* Rams #7 ("avoids being fashionable"); the manuscripts' five-hundred-year shelf life; Rapid Response as the opposite case.

- **0:** Instantly datable to its trend year: glassmorphism, mesh blobs, a grain overlay, the AI-illustration look, a trendy display face.
- **1:** Mostly timeless, with one fashionable tell.
- **2:** No obvious tells, but the palette or rendering might age.
- **3:** It relies on durable things: ink, paper, proportion, line, historical colour. Nothing about it says "2026" except perhaps that it could not have been made before code.

**Date test.** Ask: "If I saw this in 2036, could I guess the year it was made to within two years?" If yes, fail. Also ask: "Would it look dated next to the Spinola Hours?"

### 11. Point of view

*Sources:* Lista ("a meaningful collection"); Ars Electronica ("did not innovate… eliminated"); the NDA's "innovation"; Chimero (the *why*).

- **0:** A technique demo: "here is flow-field ornament."
- **1:** A coherent style without a reason.
- **2:** A clear idea, executed, but derivative of a known generative artist's look.
- **3:** A stance that can be stated in one sentence, that no one else has taken, and that every visual decision serves. Seen once, it is recognizable as this author's.

**One-sentence test.** Write what it is *about* in one sentence that does not describe the technique. Then check every visible element against that sentence. Fail if more than one element serves nothing.

### 12. Provenance and legibility of the system

*Sources:* Planetary (code, version history, licence, intent, printed master); Whitney (several AARON versions); Rhizome (restageability); MoMA (emulations when the code cannot be had).

- **0:** It works only in the author's browser. The seed and version are not recorded, and the output cannot be reproduced.
- **1:** It is reproducible from the seed, but undocumented.
- **2:** Seed, version and parameters are embedded or exportable. There is a README of intent.
- **3:** Any output can be re-rendered byte-identically from its recorded seed and version. Exported SVGs carry metadata (seed, version, date, author, licence). The change log tells the work's history. It has a written statement of intent and a fallback that degrades gracefully without JavaScript. It could be accessioned tomorrow.

**Accession test.** Could a registrar, with only the repository and one exported file, identify, re-render and explain this work in ten years? If not, fail.

## 4. Anti-patterns: what reads instantly as cheap, digital or "AI slop"

Each entry names the property it violates, so the list explains rather than just forbids.

1. **Grain or noise overlays** (a full-screen `feTurbulence` or PNG noise at 5–15% opacity).
   - *Violates:* Roughness (11) faked as a filter; Not-Separateness (15), because it sits on top of everything equally.
   - *Instead:* texture belongs to the ink and the paper, with variation in the stroke and none over the type.
2. **Uniform random jitter.** The same amplitude of `random()` on every vertex, every frame.
   - *Violates:* Levels of Scale (1), Gradients (10), Ruskin (imperfection without cause).
   - *Instead:* tie irregularity to a cause and vary its amplitude by scale. The tell is "wobbly SVG."
3. **Single-octave Perlin or simplex fields** read as "the generative look." Flow fields of uniform-length lines on a dark background are the Processing default from 2015.
   - *Violates:* Good Shape (6), Strong Centres (2), Longevity.
4. **Symmetric clip-art.** Mirror-symmetric corner ornaments copied to four corners, plus fleurons from a dingbat font.
   - *Violates:* Local Symmetries (7) without an asymmetric whole; Echoes (12); Jones 36.
5. **Gradient blobs, mesh gradients and glassmorphism.**
   - *Violates:* Boundaries (3), Good Shape (6), Contrast (9).
   - These are the canonical 2020–24 SaaS look, so they also fail the date test.
6. **Ornament as wallpaper.** A pattern behind the text, or a full-bleed texture under paragraphs.
   - *Violates:* the Void (13); Bringhurst; the remove-it test.
7. **The swappable border.** The same frame around every card regardless of content.
   - *Violates:* Not-Separateness (15) and Integration.
8. **Monoline vector strokes with round caps**, as the default output of `stroke-linecap: round`.
   - *Violates:* line quality. Every stroke has the same weight from start to end, which reads as "drawn by a plotter, unsupervised."
9. **Fake hand-drawn** filters (roughen, sketchy SVG filters) on geometric shapes.
   - *Violates:* Ruskin, because the imperfection does not come from the making.
   - The wobble is applied after the decision, so the decision still reads as mechanical.
10. **AI-illustration tells:**
    - over-rendered soft lighting;
    - everything at the same level of detail;
    - symmetrical "fantasy filigree" with no construction logic;
    - lines that start nowhere and end nowhere, failing Jones 11 (parent stem);
    - gold rendered as a yellow-brown gradient with specular highlights;
    - motifs that dissolve into mush at 400%.
11. **Parchment-texture backgrounds and faux-medieval blackletter.**
    - *Violates:* the pastiche test; Jones 36.
    - Theme-park medieval.
12. **Pastel or neon palettes with no value structure.**
    - *Violates:* Contrast (9).
    - Fails grayscale: everything is mid-tone.
13. **Perpetual ambient motion**: floating particles, drifting gradients, infinite rotation.
    - *Violates:* Behaviour, Inner Calm (14).
    - Ignores reduced motion.
14. **Re-randomizing on every load or resize**, so the page is never the same twice by accident rather than by design.
    - *Violates:* Provenance and Behaviour, because no output can be pointed to, cited or reproduced.
15. **Mobile by subtraction**: `display:none` below 768 px, or a desktop border scaled until it becomes hairlines.
    - *Violates:* Holds at every viewport.
16. **Effect stacking**: grain + glow + drop-shadow + blur on one element.
    - *Violates:* Simplicity (14), Rams #10.
17. **Over-even distribution.** Poisson-disk scatter at one density everywhere.
    - *Violates:* Strong Centres (2), Gradients (10), Positive Space (5).
    - It reads as "algorithm," not as "composition."

## 5. How to use this

- **Score every prototype in the playground with the one-page table (§0)** and record the scores beside the commit.
- **Rework rule:** fix the lowest-scoring criterion first, unless Beauty is below 3, in which case fix Beauty first.
- **Beauty is lexicographic.** A clever system, well documented and historically aware, with Beauty 2, ranks *below* a plain ornament with Beauty 3. When an idea and beauty conflict, beauty wins and the idea is reworked.
- **Every piece needs its 100-seed contact sheet and its 390 px screenshot** before it can be called finished.
- **The bar in one sentence:** beautiful at first glance; designed at every zoom level and every screen width; inseparable from its text; a system whose every output deserves keeping; imperfect only where the hand would be; and documented well enough to accession.
