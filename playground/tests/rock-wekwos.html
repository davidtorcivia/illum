Model

Proto-Indo-European has not been spoken for thousands of years, and nobody who spoke it wrote any of it down. Every PIE word we know today is a reconstruction. It is, however, the ur-language of many modern languages, and this thought was rolling around in my head while working with LLMs: the living language machines. Surely there is some echo within there? Wouldn't it be funny to pull it out? This is how I taught a language model to read and write PIE, in the scholars' notation.

The result

In the end, on the project's benchmark the model scores 88.5 lenient and 86.2 strict. The best frontier runs I tested, Claude Opus 5.5 at xhigh and GPT-6.1 Sol at high, score 86.0 and 84.4 lenient, then 50.0 and 47.0 strict. The lenient score forgives notation (accents, vowel length, ASCII spellings like h2 for h₂), and simply asks whether the grammar is right. The strict score grades the exact form, written the way the scholarly reconstructions write it. The frontier models lose over 35 points when the score demands this fidelity, while this model loses 2.3.

The lexicon

The lexicon holds over 8,300 entries. Over 7,400 come directly from the well-sourced work of Wiktionary's reconstructions (roots, words and the derivatives in their descendant trees), over 800 are from LIV², the standard lexicon of PIE verbs, 135 are from the Nomina im Indogermanischen Lexikon and 3 were added by hand from scouring handbooks. LIV² and NIL don't exist as digital data, so I had them transcribed from page images: 1,172 roots from LIV², and 207 roots with 3,044 formations from all 737 pages of NIL. Where two transcriptions of the same page overlapped, they agreed 96% of the time.

A lexicon this size does carry errors (as do the sources), and the project made considerable efforts to find, correct, and source our corrections for these errors.
