Book I

Chapter I. Of the initial, the paraph and the ends of lines.

But our higher instincts are not deceived. We take no pleasure in the building provided for us, resembling that which we take in a new book or a new picture. We may be proud of its size, complacent in its correctness, and happy in its convenience. We may take the same pleasure in its symmetry and workmanship as in a well-ordered room, or a skilful piece of manufacture.

And this we suppose to be all the pleasure that architecture was ever intended to give us. The idea of reading a building as we would read Milton or Dante never enters our minds.

Yet all this is true, and self-evident; only hidden from us, as many other self-evident things are, by false teaching. Nothing is a great work of art, for the production of which either rules or models can be given.

Exactly so far as architecture works on known rules, and from given models, it is not an art, but a manufacture; and of the two procedures, the latter is the less noble.

Let us then understand at once, that change or variety is as much a necessity to the human heart and brain in buildings as in books; that there is no merit, though there is some occasional use, in monotony.

Chapter II. Of the frames.

The scribe’s page was ruled before a word was written, and its proportions were not arbitrary.

The pilcrow. The scribe left a gap at the head of a paragraph for the rubricator to paint a ¶ in red. When the rubricator ran out of time the gap stayed empty.

An ornament that was never painted.

Nothing here covers or crowds a word. Take the marks away and the page is complete; put them back and it is a place.

Book II

Chapter I. Of rank.

Ornament that marks everything marks nothing. In the book the ladder was strict: a painted initial opened a book, a penwork initial a chapter, a paraph a paragraph.

A reader could count the parts of an opening at a glance, because the marks were spent by rank.

Outside the chapters, by markup alone: a paraph on this paragraph, a filler after it.
