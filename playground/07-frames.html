Finding one’s way

Notes on the ornament that made a book a place

The page as a map

A medieval book had no page numbers, no index and no search. Its readers found their way by its ornament: the large initial that opened a book, the smaller one at a chapter, the paraph at a paragraph, the filler that closed a short line. Every mark said where you were and how much it mattered, and it said so at arm’s length, before any reading began.

The same marks can be grown on a web page from the page’s own structure, so that the ornament is the information architecture, not a costume over it. That asks something of the maker that Ruskin asked of the builders of his day.

You must either make a tool of the creature, or a man of him. You cannot make both. Men were not intended to work with the accuracy of tools, to be precise and perfect in all their actions. If you will have that precision out of them, and make their fingers measure degrees like cog-wheels, and their arms strike curves like compasses, you must unhumanize them.

John Ruskin, The Stones of Venice, 1853

A program cannot be a man, but it can refuse to be a cog-wheel: it can let every line it draws keep a little of the hand’s irregularity, and spend its precision where a reader needs it, on where things begin and end.

The measure of the page

The scribe’s page was ruled before a word was written. Its proportions were not arbitrary: in the books that look most at rest, the text block sits in the page as the page sits in the spread, and the margins grow from the gutter outward, the foot the widest of all.

A web page has no fixed page, but it has a measure, and the measure can be honoured in the same way: the ornament sits in the margins the text leaves, and when the margins disappear on a phone, the ornament changes form instead of shrinking.

The pilcrow. The scribe left a gap at the head of a paragraph for the rubricator to paint a ¶ in red. When the rubricator ran out of time the gap stayed empty, and the empty gap became the paragraph indent: our most familiar typographic convention is an ornament that was never painted.

Rank, not decoration

Ornament that marks everything marks nothing. In the book the ladder was strict: a painted initial opened a book, a penwork initial a chapter, a paraph a paragraph. A reader could count the parts of an opening at a glance because the marks were spent by rank.

Our most invisible typographic convention is an ornament that was never painted.

On the web the ranks are already in the markup: headings, sections, quotations, figures, code, asides, navigation. The ornament only has to read them, and to spend itself where the reader will need to find their way.

Grown from the page

Each frame on this page is measured from its box when the page is laid out, and drawn again when it changes. The contents, being the map, carries the most; an aside, the least.

// a frame for every box, grown from its own box
for (const el of boxes(document))
  draw(frameFor(el, measure(el), { rich, seed }));

Nothing here covers or crowds a word. Take the frames away and the page is complete; put them back and it is a place.
